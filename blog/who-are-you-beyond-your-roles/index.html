Who Are You Beneath the Roles You've Been Playing?

By Kay Joy Flowers

Published 18 November 2026

Your roles describe parts of your life; they do not contain everything you are. When being the teacher, mother, partner or reliable one has taken most of your attention, reconnecting with yourself can begin through ordinary choices: what you value, what interests you and what you no longer want to agree to automatically.

Ask someone who they are and they will often tell you what they do. Ask again and you may hear who needs them.

The capable one. The sensible one. The one who keeps everything moving.

Those descriptions may be accurate. They may also leave out the person who has been doing all that carrying.

So let the question have a little more room.

Who are you when you are not explaining your usefulness?

A role can be meaningful and still become too small

There is nothing wrong with loving your work, your family or the responsibilities you have chosen. A role can give life shape. It can hold devotion, skill and genuine pleasure.

The difficulty comes when it becomes the only answer available.

Perhaps you know exactly what everyone else prefers and find it surprisingly difficult to name what you would choose. Perhaps a free afternoon feels less like freedom than a missing instruction. Perhaps a change in work or family life has removed the familiar answer to “Who am I?”

You do not have to reject what mattered to you. You can begin by noticing what the role has expressed, and what has had to wait outside it.

A teacher may love discovery but be tired of constant evaluation. A mother may cherish her children and want uninterrupted time to create. A reliable person may enjoy contributing and want someone else to take a turn.

Those things can belong in the same life.

Remembrance begins with your own attention

Who Are You? begins with a question people have asked me after hearing one of my creative ideas. The manuscript opens outward from my story into the reader's own inquiry: what might you recognise about yourself when you look at your life through another lens?

This is part of what I mean by remembrance in A Living Cosmology of Remembrance. I use it for the return to a relationship with your own life—your perception, imagination, body, choices and sense of meaning.

It is a philosophical and creative invitation. You do not need to adopt a spiritual explanation to explore it. If spiritual language belongs to your life, there is room for that too.

The question remains yours. An author can offer a doorway. She cannot decide who must walk through it.

Separate the role from the rule

Sometimes it is not the role itself that exhausts you. It is a rule that has attached itself to the role.

“I am a good friend” becomes “I must always be available”.

“I care about my students” becomes “I must absorb every demand”.

“I am strong” becomes “I must never need anyone”.

Write down a role that matters to you. Underneath it, finish the sentence: To be allowed to remain this person, I believe I have to…

Look carefully at what you wrote. Did you choose that condition? Is it actually required? What happens to you when you try to meet it all the time?

You may discover that you want to keep the love, the craft or the contribution and revise the terms under which you give it.

That is a substantial act of authorship.

An exercise: let “I am” become larger

The Who Are You? manuscript includes an invitation to write from “I am”. Here is a grounded way to begin that inquiry.

Begin with the familiar answers

Write the roles that come easily: educator, parent, partner, carer, organiser. Include the unofficial ones, such as peacemaker or problem-solver.

You are acknowledging them, not crossing them out.

Add what you value

Try a sentence such as “I value honesty”, “I care about beauty”, or “I want room for curiosity”. Use your own language, including words that are less polished and more accurate.

Notice whether you are naming a value you live by or one you think a good person should name. You can take your time with the difference.

Add what attracts your attention

What do you notice without being instructed? Colour? Stories? The way a room feels? How something works? A question nobody has asked?

You do not need to turn that interest into a business, qualification or new identity. You can let it belong to you first.

Give one answer a place in the day

If you value quiet, make room for a quiet moment. If you miss creating, put a few words or marks on a page. If you have been agreeing automatically, give yourself time before the next answer.

Keep the action proportionate. You are giving a preference somewhere to exist.

You do not need a replacement performance

There is a tempting shortcut here: retire one demanding identity and replace it with a more impressive one.

Now you must be the sovereign woman. The awakened woman. The woman who never doubts herself, never needs reassurance and always knows what she wants.

That sounds exhausting.

There is room to be uncertain. There is room to change your mind after trying something. There is room for a preference that surprises people who knew an earlier version of you.

In my work on sovereignty, personal authority includes the ordinary act of taking your own experience seriously. It does not require a flawless new character.

Questions about identity and roles

How do I find myself when I do not know what I want?

Start with a small choice that is available now. Notice what interests you, what you would like less of and what you would try without needing to be good at it. Specific preferences can be easier to hear than a demand to discover your whole purpose.

Can I change without abandoning my responsibilities?

Yes. You can question an automatic rule, share a task or make space for an interest while continuing to care about your commitments. Some changes need conversation and practical arrangements. They do not all require a dramatic exit.

What if a role I loved has ended?

You can miss it. Recognising that you are more than a role does not erase its significance. Give yourself room to name what it held—connection, rhythm, contribution—and consider how those qualities might find expression again.
