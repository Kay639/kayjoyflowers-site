What Does Frequency Have to Do With Healing?

By Kay Joy Flowers

Published 18 November 2026

Frequency describes how often something repeats; in sound, it is measured in hertz. In conversations about healing, the word can refer to medical technologies, music or the felt quality of a personal or spiritual experience. Distinguishing those meanings helps us explore the subject with clarity.

A piece of music changes the atmosphere of a room. A familiar voice gives you space to breathe. An ultrasound machine uses sound to produce an image inside the body.

All three may appear in a conversation about frequency. They ask different questions and offer different kinds of evidence.

In Frequency — The Future of Medicine, I explore how to keep curiosity and discernment in the same conversation. There is room for the person who wants to understand a medical instrument and the person who wants to describe what singing means in her life.

Clarity lets each experience keep its own significance.

What is frequency?

If something completes 440 cycles in one second, its frequency is 440 hertz, written 440 Hz. In sound, frequency helps determine pitch. Loudness involves other properties, including the strength of the sound wave.

A number alone does not tell you what an experience or treatment will do. The kind of wave, its intensity, duration and delivery all matter. So does what the intervention is intended to achieve.

The word resonance also has a physical meaning: a system can respond strongly to a driving frequency near one of its natural modes. In ordinary language, “that resonates with me” means something feels meaningful or familiar. Both uses can communicate something valuable when we are clear about which one we mean.

Where frequency has an established role in medicine

Ultrasound is a concrete example. It uses high-frequency sound waves for medical imaging. Some therapeutic forms deliver carefully controlled energy to tissue for defined purposes. The US National Institutes of Health describes diagnostic and therapeutic ultrasound, including focused applications.

The relevant evidence belongs to the particular technology and use. A clinical ultrasound system has a defined exposure, target and procedure. That cannot be transferred to a consumer recording simply because both involve sound.

The useful question is: What exactly is being delivered, for what purpose, and how was its effect established?

That question allows real developments to be understood on their own terms.

What does research say about music and wellbeing?

Music is more than a frequency label. It includes rhythm, melody, tempo, memory, preference and the circumstances in which we listen.

The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health summarises research suggesting benefits from some music-based interventions for outcomes such as anxiety, pain and subjective sleep quality. Results vary by condition and study, and listening to a recording is not the same service as working with a trained music therapist.

A calmer interval during a difficult day matters. It can be described honestly without requiring a claim that the music has cured an underlying condition.

You can ask a practical question of your own listening: Does this help me settle, feel connected or enjoy the moment? The answer is useful at that scale.

What about 432 Hz and 528 Hz?

These numbers often appear on recordings described as healing music. They need a little unpacking.

432 Hz: usually a tuning reference

For a recording labelled 432 Hz, the number commonly refers to the reference tuning for the note A. The recording still contains many notes and frequencies.

My Frequency manuscript discusses the difference between a listening preference and a treatment claim. You can prefer a particular tuning without needing to conclude that it is the correct frequency for every body.

When evaluating a claim, look for the actual comparison. Was the music otherwise identical? Was the volume comparable? Were listeners assessed beyond the session? A number in the title does not answer those questions.

528 Hz: what a small study measured

A 2018 exploratory study by Akimoto and colleagues involved nine healthy adults listening to two music conditions on separate days. It reported changes in some short-term stress-related measures after the condition described as 528 Hz music.

That is a small, specific observation. The study did not measure DNA repair or establish a treatment for disease. Its music was tuned using an A reference of 444 Hz so that the scale included a note near 528 Hz; it was not simply one continuous 528 Hz tone.

If you enjoy the music, you can enjoy it. A biological claim needs evidence for the biological change being claimed.

What is still being investigated?

Researchers are studying precisely timed sensory stimulation, among other approaches. A 2022 feasibility and pilot study examined 40 Hz light-and-sound stimulation, including a randomised pilot involving 15 people with mild probable Alzheimer's dementia.

It investigated safety, feasibility and exploratory outcomes. A study at that scale opens further research questions; it does not establish that an ordinary 40 Hz audio track treats Alzheimer's disease.

Here, 40 Hz describes the timing of stimulation in a research protocol. It should not be confused with a musical tuning reference. Numbers that share the unit “hertz” may describe very different things.

Where do traditional and spiritual practices belong?

Chant, song and ritual can hold meaning within particular traditions and communities. Their significance may involve relationship, devotion, memory, participation or a sense of the sacred.

Calling all of these practices “frequency medicine” can flatten those differences. It can also make a spiritual practice sound as though it has been tested as a clinical intervention when it has not.

My own language of frequency sometimes describes the quality of being in a space or in relationship with myself. That is a perspective I bring to the subject. It belongs alongside a clear account of what is measured and what is personally experienced.

Wonder does not need a borrowed laboratory coat.

My perspective: attention remains yours

In the manuscript, I describe buying Genius Insight in 2024 and learning with two practitioners. My inquiry includes what happens when a screen offers categories, colours and numbers: how do I respond, and how much authority do I give it?

I also offer Genius Insight energetic scans as complementary wellness work. A wellness output is not a medical finding. The clinical research discussed above does not validate that service as a diagnostic test or disease treatment.

The distinction is part of the sovereignty I care about. You can ask what something means. You can notice your experience. You can ask for the evidence behind a claim and keep appropriate healthcare in place.

How to explore sound in an ordinary way

Choose music you already enjoy and listen at a comfortable volume. Notice the experience before and afterwards without demanding a dramatic result. Perhaps you feel more settled; perhaps you would prefer silence.

Let that observation inform your next choice. Stop if the sound feels unpleasant or distressing. A listening practice can accompany care, while symptoms and treatment decisions belong with a qualified clinician.

Questions about frequency and healing

Is there one best healing frequency?

There is no single frequency established as best for every person or condition. A clinical intervention needs a specific target and protocol. For everyday listening, your comfort and preference are useful places to begin.

Does 528 Hz music repair DNA?

The small human listening study discussed here did not test DNA repair. It measured short-term stress-related outcomes. Those results do not support a claim that a recording repairs human DNA.

Can a spiritual experience still matter without a medical claim?

Yes. Meaning, connection and personal reflection can matter on their own terms. Describing an experience accurately allows it to keep that value without turning it into a promise of treatment.
