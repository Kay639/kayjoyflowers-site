Why Teachers Are Exhausted Beyond Burnout

By Kay Joy Flowers

Published 18 November 2026

Teacher exhaustion can reflect sustained workload, emotional demands, repeated change and too little opportunity to recover. When responsibility keeps expanding, being told to become more resilient can feel like one more task. Understanding the working conditions and the personal cost makes a better starting point than asking why an individual teacher is not coping.

The teaching day ends. Your attention does not necessarily leave with you.

There are decisions to revisit, preparation to finish and messages waiting. Even a quiet evening can contain a classroom's worth of unfinished concerns.

I worked in education for more than 35 years. In Fearlessly Unfuckwithable, I write about overwork, emotional labour and the experience of continuing to function while my own needs became harder to hear.

That history informs the question I bring here: what happens when being the person who keeps going becomes the only version of you the system makes room for?

Burnout describes more than being tired

The World Health Organization describes burnout in relation to chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. Its description includes exhaustion, distance or cynicism towards work, and reduced professional efficacy. WHO classifies it as an occupational phenomenon.

“Survival mode” is a more informal expression. I use it here for the experience of organising the day around getting through demands while your own capacity receives less and less attention. It is not a diagnosis or a further clinical stage of burnout.

The phrase can be useful when it helps you recognise a pattern: always preparing, always responding, struggling to stop, and finding little room for anything beyond recovery from the working day.

The name matters less than what the pattern is costing you.

What New Zealand research tells us about the load

NZEI Te Riu Roa's April 2026 report on curriculum changes drew on responses representing 7,780 individuals across more than 700 primary schools. It was gathered through worksite representatives and school staff groups to support bargaining claims.

In its summary of the findings, NZEI reported that 87.5% of primary teachers described an increased workload and 83.2% of respondents felt overwhelmed by the pace and extent of change. These are findings from that survey, not a count of every teacher in the country.

The New Zealand Council for Educational Research's report on secondary teachers in its 2025 national survey adds another perspective. It reports that 31% disagreed that their workload was manageable and 35% disagreed that their stress was manageable. It also notes that most teachers enjoyed their jobs.

That combination matters. Enjoying teaching and struggling with its demands can be true at the same time.

Why “be more resilient” can miss the point

Resilience can include adapting, seeking support and recovering after difficulty. Those are useful capacities.

But the word becomes less useful when it means absorbing every extra task without allowing the cost to become visible.

A teacher who continues producing work may look as though they have capacity for more. What is less visible is what made the output possible: a cancelled walk, another evening of preparation, a meal eaten while answering messages, or personal plans repeatedly postponed.

These are everyday examples of the trade-offs the question invites us to notice. They are not a measure of anyone's dedication.

In “Resilience Without Self-Abandonment”, my sovereignty work asks what happens when endurance becomes a reason to stop listening to yourself. Continuing is one possible choice. Reducing, renegotiating and asking for support are choices too.

Look at the demand before judging your response

Take one recurring moment of strain. Be specific: the extra documentation, the constant messages, the task that has no clear limit.

Then separate three things.

The requirement: What has actually been requested, by whom, and by when?

The added expectation: What are you assuming you must do beyond that? This might be a school norm, an unclear instruction or your own wish to do the work well.

The cost: What time and energy does it require, and what gives way to accommodate it?

Some requirements will remain. Some expectations can be clarified. Some demands need to be addressed collectively rather than absorbed privately.

The purpose is to make the situation visible enough for a real conversation.

What can help during the day?

Make one transition less crowded

Where supervision and the timetable allow, use a brief transition to notice your feet against the floor and look at three things around you. You can leave your breathing natural.

This is a small adaptation of Feet on the Floor from my Pause Library. It offers a moment to arrive in the space you are actually in. It does not need to become another wellbeing target.

For a fuller version, use the practical guide to overwhelm.

Ask for prioritisation in concrete terms

A useful sentence is: “With the time available, I can complete A or B by Friday. Which should take priority?”

Another is: “What can be removed or delayed to make room for this?”

These questions make the trade-off explicit. They also give the person assigning work something concrete to respond to.

Put recurring pressure somewhere it can be addressed

Bring a pattern to an appropriate colleague, leader, union representative or workplace support channel. A short record of the demand and its time cost can make the discussion more specific.

You should not have to construct a perfect argument before your experience deserves attention. The record is there to help describe the work, not to prove your worth.

Your identity deserves room beyond teaching

When teaching holds much of your purpose, reducing what you give can feel like reducing who you are.

But care for learners does not require every part of your life to become available to work. Your creativity, relationships, rest and private interests belong in the picture too.

The question “Who are you beneath your roles?” can be particularly useful here. It makes room to keep what you love about teaching while reconsidering what it is allowed to consume.

Questions about teacher exhaustion

Can I be burned out if I still love teaching?

Enjoyment of the work does not make excessive demands manageable. Notice exhaustion, your relationship with work and the effect on daily life rather than using love of teaching as a reason to dismiss the cost.

Will breathing exercises fix teacher burnout?

A brief practice may help you pause and notice what you need. Workload, staffing, expectations and support also need attention. Personal practices cannot take responsibility for the conditions under which people work.

What if rest is not helping?

Persistent exhaustion, sleep problems or difficulty functioning deserve a conversation with a qualified health professional. You can seek care while also addressing work demands; you do not have to settle on a diagnosis first.

Explore sovereignty without another performance target

Fearlessly Unfuckwithable is my exploration of self-trust, capacity, boundaries and the decision to stop abandoning yourself. It offers a personal companion to this inquiry, with space to recognise what your life has been asking of you.
