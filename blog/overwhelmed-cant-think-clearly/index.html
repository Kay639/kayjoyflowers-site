What to Do When You're Overwhelmed and Can't Think Clearly

By Kay Joy Flowers

Published 18 November 2026

When you are overwhelmed and cannot think clearly, reduce what you are trying to process. Pause one source of input, notice the support beneath your body, and choose one small next step. You do not have to solve the whole situation before making the next moment more manageable.

If this is the moment you are in, begin here: put down one thing you do not need to hold while reading. Let this be the only page for a moment.

There is no need to perform calm. You are making a little room.

Why does everything feel urgent?

An unfinished task, a message, a decision and someone else's expectation can all compete for attention. When you try to hold them together, deciding where to start becomes another demand.

The Australian Veteran and Family Wellbeing Agency describes racing thoughts, difficulty focusing and trouble beginning tasks as possible experiences of overwhelm. Its guidance includes grounding, reducing the load and breaking tasks into smaller steps.

My Pause Library begins with a similarly practical question: what kind of pause can you use in the state you are in?

The three practices below are shortened written adaptations of the approved Overwhelmed collection: Feet on the Floor, The Long Exhale, and One Next Thing. You can choose one. There is no sequence you have to complete.

1. Feet on the Floor: when you need somewhere to begin

Start with contact rather than an explanation.

  1. Sit or stand in a position that works for you. Notice where your feet meet the floor, your socks or your shoes. If your feet cannot reach the floor, notice the support of your seat or bed instead.
  2. If comfortable, press your feet down gently. Release the pressure and notice the difference. You can repeat this once without forcing it.
  3. Look around and name three things you can see.
  4. Notice two things you can hear.
  5. Notice one thing against your skin: your clothes, the air or the surface supporting you.

You do not need to feel transformed. Notice whether you have a little more contact with the room you are in.

At a desk, this can be quiet and unobtrusive. You do not need music, a special posture or an explanation for anyone else. The point is to give your attention something immediate and ordinary to meet.

If paying attention inside your body feels uncomfortable, stay with what you can see around you. You choose how much attention to give and where to place it.

2. The Long Exhale: when a gentle rhythm would help

The approved practice uses a comfortable in-breath for four counts and an out-breath for six, without holding the breath.

  1. Let your shoulders rest as comfortably as they can.
  2. Breathe in gently through your nose: one, two, three, four.
  3. Let the breath out softly through your mouth: six, five, four, three, two, one.
  4. Repeat for up to three comfortable rounds, then return to your natural breathing.

Keep the breathing easy. If counting, nose breathing or the longer exhale feels uncomfortable, let the pattern go and breathe normally. If you feel dizzy or more distressed, stop the exercise and return your attention to the room.

There is no prize for a larger breath. You are not trying to force a particular state or finish a breathing assignment. You are trying a rhythm and noticing whether it suits you today.

You can also skip breathwork altogether and use Feet on the Floor.

3. One Next Thing: when the whole day is in your head

Take a piece of paper or open a note. Give yourself enough space to write without making the page presentable.

Put the circling thoughts on the page

Write the tasks, worries and decisions as they come. You are not sorting yet. A messy list is enough.

Identify what actually belongs to today

Ask: Of everything here, what truly has to happen today?

Circle those items. If nothing has to happen today, allow that to be an answer. The feeling of urgency and an actual deadline are different pieces of information.

Choose the first physical action

Make it smaller than the project.

“Sort out my work” is too large to begin. “Open the email that contains the deadline” is an action. “Write the first sentence of my reply” is another.

Write one clear sentence describing the step you choose.

Check whose task it is

Is this yours to do? Can someone help? Does another person need to make the decision before you can proceed?

If help is needed, the next action may be asking for it. If the task belongs elsewhere, write the relevant person's name beside it.

Make the step easier

What would help: a quieter place, water, fewer tabs, a short timer, or the information you are missing?

Read your one sentence back. Then do that step, or take the rest you have recognised you need. The list can hold the other things while your attention returns to one.

A pause can reveal what needs to change

After a little space, you may realise the problem is the amount you are carrying. The answer may include cancelling something, clarifying an expectation or sharing a task.

The pause has done useful work if it helps you recognise your capacity. It does not have to make an unreasonable load feel reasonable.

If work in education is the source of that load, the article on teacher exhaustion looks at the relationship between personal steadiness and working conditions.

Questions about overwhelm

What if I still cannot think clearly after a pause?

Reduce the demand further. You may need food, rest, help with a decision or a different environment. Persistent difficulty concentrating or managing everyday life deserves attention from a qualified health professional.

Do I have to do all three practices?

No. Choose one, or use only part of one. These written adaptations are invitations, not a programme to complete correctly.

Is sudden confusion the same as overwhelm?

Sudden confusion—such as becoming disoriented or unable to understand what is happening—needs urgent medical assessment. It is different from familiar task overload. The NHS guidance on sudden confusion explains why it should not be managed with a grounding exercise alone.
