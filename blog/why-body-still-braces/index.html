Why Does Your Body Still Brace When Life Is Getting Better?

By Kay Joy Flowers

Published 18 November 2026

Your body can remain tense, watchful or ready for the next problem even after your circumstances improve. Stress, uncertainty and experiences of unreliable support can shape what you expect next. Recognising that life has changed and feeling able to settle into that change do not always happen together.

The room is quiet. The difficult conversation is over. Something that was uncertain has finally become more stable.

And still, your shoulders are up. Your jaw is working. You are mentally preparing for a problem that has not arrived.

Then comes the second layer: What is wrong with me? I should be enjoying this.

There is another place to begin. Notice the bracing without making it a verdict on your ability to live.

What does “bracing” actually mean?

Here, bracing means the everyday experience of holding yourself ready: tightening, checking, anticipating, struggling to stop. It describes an experience rather than a diagnosis.

It might look like finishing one urgent task and immediately searching for the next. It might be the difficulty of sitting down without finding a reason to get up. Or the way a generous offer sends you straight into calculating what it will cost you later.

The NHS describes tension, difficulty relaxing and problems concentrating among possible features of anxiety. It also recognises that positive life changes can bring stress. That gives us useful context; it does not tell us the cause of every tight shoulder or restless evening.

The useful question is specific: What am I preparing for in this moment?

Why can improvement feel unfamiliar?

If you have repeatedly had to adjust to sudden demands, you may have become very skilled at anticipating them. That skill does not necessarily switch off because one circumstance changes.

You may trust a new situation in principle while still checking it in practice. You may have a quieter home and an unchanged workload. You may have received good news while still carrying fatigue from the months before it.

“Things are better” can be true without meaning “everything that strained me has ended”.

In Fearlessly Unfuckwithable, I explore the gap between understanding freedom and being able to inhabit it. In the chapter “The Embodied Experience”, I describe how readily I overrode fatigue and unease in favour of what I thought I should be able to do.

That recognition changed the question. Instead of asking how to make myself perform ease, I began paying attention to what I was actually experiencing.

First, distinguish an old expectation from a present demand

Before trying to calm yourself, look at what is happening around you.

Is someone still ignoring your boundaries? Are you waiting for an important decision? Are you exhausted because the work has not stopped? Is there a practical need that deserves attention?

A body under continuing pressure does not need an argument about why it ought to feel safe. It needs the pressure taken seriously.

You can also ask: What am I predicting that is not happening right now?

Perhaps the room really is quiet. Perhaps nobody has asked you for anything. Perhaps the person offering help has been clear about what they can give.

Name those details. “There is no message I need to answer this evening” is more concrete than demanding that you trust life forever.

Try a smaller experiment than “just relax”

You do not have to make your whole body soften. Begin with a moment you can actually meet.

Notice one point of support

Feel the chair beneath you or the contact between your feet and the floor. Keep your eyes open if that suits you. Notice one ordinary object in the room.

There is no correct sensation to produce. You are simply allowing some attention to rest on what is here alongside the anticipation.

Name what is happening without a character judgement

“I notice I am checking my phone again.”

“My shoulders are tight.”

“I am expecting this good thing to disappear.”

These are observations. “I ruin everything” is a sentence that closes the inquiry before it begins.

Choose one response that includes you

You might put the phone down while you finish your tea. You might ask what an offer includes. You might decide that the next task can wait until morning.

Make the choice small enough that you can recognise it happening. You are practising participation in your own life, including your capacity and preferences in the decision.

If everything feels too crowded to choose, the Pause Library practices for overwhelm offer a more guided starting point.

Let ease be ordinary

Sometimes the pressure comes from believing a good moment must prove something. If you can enjoy this afternoon, perhaps you will finally be healed. If you become anxious again, perhaps all your progress was imaginary.

An afternoon does not need that job.

You can enjoy the warmth of a cup and still have questions about tomorrow. You can receive support and still want the terms made clear. You can rest without declaring that uncertainty has ended.

Sovereignty, as I use the word, includes being able to choose while feelings are still present. You do not need to become permanently calm before your judgement counts.

Questions you may be asking

Why do I feel anxious when things are going well?

Positive change can bring uncertainty, and earlier experiences may influence what you expect. There may also be pressure that has not ended. Start by noticing the situation and the expectation separately, rather than assuming you are sabotaging something good.

Does feeling tense mean I have trauma?

Tension alone cannot tell you that. Bracing is a description, not a diagnosis. If symptoms persist, disturb sleep or interfere with daily life, a qualified health professional can help you understand what is happening.

Do I have to feel safe before making any changes?

No. You can make a considered choice while feeling uncertain. A useful starting point is one manageable action, with enough support and room to reconsider.
